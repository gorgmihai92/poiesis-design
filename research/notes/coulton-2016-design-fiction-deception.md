# Design Fiction: Does the search for plausibility lead to deception? (Coulton, Lindley & Akmal, 2016)

- **Source:** Proceedings of DRS 2016: Future-Focused Thinking · https://doi.org/10.21606/drs.2016.148
  (pages = PDF pages of the repository copy)
- **File:** none kept. Read from Lancaster University's repository copy (eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/78563).
- **Licence:** CC BY-NC 4.0, stated on the first page.
- **Evidence:** conceptual, with cases · a reflection on four design fictions (one of them an earlier paper by two of
  the authors), a 1938 radio play and a fiction built for this paper (p. 3–12). No experiment and no count of how
  many people were misled.
- **Read:** full text, p. 1–14, on 2026-11-22. The reference list was skimmed.

## What it says
- The authors sort design fictions by how they present themselves: obviously fiction, identified as fiction, and
  ambiguous or concealed (p. 3).
- A fiction that says it is one can still be taken as real. The authors' fictional research paper named itself as
  design fiction in its keywords and in its conclusion, and reviewers and listeners did not fully grasp that it was
  fiction; it was written in the form of a real paper (p. 5).
- A 1938 radio play announced that it was fiction four times, and some listeners still believed it (p. 5).
- What misleads is the form: an artefact that copies a familiar form is read as that form, whatever its makers
  intend (p. 6, 14).
- Two designers told the press that a speculative product was headed for production because the truth drew no
  coverage (p. 7). The authors call that deliberate deceit and say it is rare (p. 14).
- After the authors posted an unlabelled video of their own fiction, a filmmaker took the product for real, and it
  took them considerable effort to convince him otherwise (p. 12).
- Their answer to the title's question is yes: the search for plausibility can lead to deception, and often
  does (p. 13).
- Deception in a study is accepted in some fields when the risk is negligible and it is revealed afterwards (p. 3).

## What it means for the plugin
- One line in `explore` is not about design: when a sketch is shown to people, they are told it is a prototype.
  The cases above are why the plugin puts the word on the sketch itself and at the top of the board, and why the
  person showing it also says so: a label beside a familiar-looking form was missed more than once.
- The paper does not test whether saying so works. It reports cases where it did not. The line is a product
  decision informed by those cases.
- The paper is about fictions set in imagined worlds. A working sketch of an interface is a nearer and plainer
  thing; that the same confusion arises with it is this plugin's assumption.
