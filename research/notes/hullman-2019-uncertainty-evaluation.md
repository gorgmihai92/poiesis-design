  (pages = PDF pages of the authors' copy)
- **File:** none kept. Read from the authors' lab copy (mucollective.northwestern.edu), labelled as the InfoVis 2018

- **Licence:** © IEEE: not stored.
- **Evidence:** survey of studies · 86 user studies of uncertainty visualizations, published 1987 to 2018, coded

- **Read:** part of the text: p. 1 and p. 7–9 of 11 (abstract, introduction, three of the findings, the

## What it says
- The survey is about how uncertainty displays are evaluated, not about which display to draw (p. 1).
- Most studies measure whether people read a value correctly. Judging accuracy was about ten times as common as
  judging the quality of a decision (p. 7).
- Reading a probability correctly does not show that a person can use it in a decision (p. 8).
- A person's stated confidence is not a direct function of statistical confidence. Reports of confidence are noisy
  and shift with the difficulty of the task, experience and effort, so a study that treats more confidence as
  better has to say why (p. 8).
- Few studies compare a display with showing no uncertainty, or with stating it in text; the authors note that a
  visualization can present more than the task needs (p. 9).
- Most studies seek to confirm that one display is better and do not try to explain why (p. 9).
- Error bars are named, with references, as a format that leads to biased reading and to uncertainty being given
  too little weight (p. 1).

## What it means for the plugin
- No rule about how to draw uncertainty rests on this article: it gives none.
- Rule U7 takes one thing from it: stated confidence is not a direct function of statistical confidence (p. 8),
  so the report shows a challenger's confidence in its words, never as a number, a bar or a tint. That last step is
  a product decision. The survey's remark that few studies compare a display with text (p. 9) is not used as a
  reason for anything.
- The patterns here have not been evaluated at all, for accuracy or for decisions. The report's rules say so.
