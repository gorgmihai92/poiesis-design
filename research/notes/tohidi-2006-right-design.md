## What it says
- Three paper prototypes of a home thermostat, made by one team with the same functions and different styles, were
  each tried by 12 people who saw only that one; 12 more people tried all three (p. 2–3). Participants were students
  or recent graduates of one university (p. 2).
- Every design was rated higher when seen alone than when seen with the other two: 9.08 against 8.13, 8.83 against
  8.39 and 7.92 against 6.89 on a ten-point scale. The middle difference was not significant (p. 5).
- People made fewer positive comments about a design seen among three, for all three designs. They made more
  negative comments only about the design rated lowest (p. 5–6).
- Seeing three designs did not bring more suggestions for improving them (p. 6, 8). The authors' conclusion: a
  usability test finds problems; it does not produce design ideas (p. 9).
- None of the 36 people who saw one design rejected it. Three of the 12 who saw three rejected one of them, and
  others named the one they would choose (p. 9).
- The authors say they cannot tell which comments were the valid ones; their working assumption is that reactions
  to three designs are the better informed (p. 7).

## What it means for the plugin
- When sketches are shown to people, several are shown, never one alone: a design seen alone is praised more and
  never rejected in this study. `explore` puts its variants side by side for that reason.
- Asking the people who try the sketches how to improve them is not expected to yield much; what they reject and
  why is the useful part.
- Limits: one product, paper prototypes, 12 people in each group, students. The comment counts come from six
  people in each group.
